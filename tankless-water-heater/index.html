How a tankless heater works and what it needs

When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor detects water moving and the unit lights a gas burner or energizes electric elements. The water passes through a heat exchanger and leaves at the set temperature. No flow, no heating.

That design makes demands on the house. A gas model draws far more gas at once than a tank burner, so it often needs a larger line, and a power-vented exhaust path. An electric model can need a very large circuit, which some older panels cannot supply.

Descaling and flushing: the maintenance that matters

Minerals in water leave scale on the heat exchanger. Scale narrows the passages and makes the unit work harder, and over time it triggers error codes, noise and weak hot water. A flush circulates a descaling solution through the unit to dissolve the buildup.

How often you need one depends on your water. The Horsham Water and Sewer Authority publishes its hardness at about 200 to 350 mg/L as calcium carbonate, averaging near 275 mg/L, or 16 grains per gallon. That is on the hard side, so we set the flush interval for your unit with scale in mind, and annual flushing is a sensible starting point unless the manufacturer says otherwise.

Error codes and common faults

Every brand writes its own codes, so we do not guess from a number alone. We read the manual for your model, check the symptom and test the part the code points to.

Ignition or flame failure

A dirty flame rod, low gas pressure or a blocked vent can stop the burner from lighting or staying lit.

Overheat or high temperature

Scale on the heat exchanger or a failing sensor can trip a shutdown.

Venting or exhaust fault

A blocked or damaged vent pipe, or a clogged condensate line on a condensing unit, causes this.

No hot water at low flow

The unit needs a minimum flow to fire. A clogged inlet screen or worn fixture can hold it below that.

Hot, cold, hot swings

Often a mixing problem from a recirculation loop, a cross-connection or too little flow. We trace which.

Leaks at the unit

Fittings, isolation valves and heat exchangers can leak. Shut off water and power and call, and see our water heater repair page for what to do while you wait.

Installing a tankless water heater

Replacing a tank with a tankless usually means more than swapping the unit. We size it to your peak flow and the incoming water temperature, which in a Pennsylvania winter can be much colder than in summer. We then check the gas line, the vent route, the condensate drain and the electrical supply.

Gas piping and venting follow the manufacturer instructions. The Township permit page lists water heaters as permit work, so ask Code Enforcement at (215) 643-3131, open Monday to Friday, 8:30 to 4:30, who files it for a tankless job. For a planned job of any kind, our water heater installation page covers the shared steps, including removal of the old tank.

What tankless does well

  • Steady hot water as long as flow stays within its rating
  • Frees the floor space a tank would take
  • No standby heat loss from a stored tank
  • Many models are rated for a longer service life than a tank

What to weigh against it

  • Higher purchase and installation cost
  • May need a bigger gas line or electrical upgrade
  • Needs flushing on a regular schedule
  • Hot water can fail in a power cut because of the controls and fan

What affects the price of tankless water heater service

Costs split into the unit, the infrastructure around it and the upkeep.

Model and capacity

Higher flow ratings and condensing models cost more than basic units.

Gas line or circuit upgrade

If the existing line or panel cannot support the unit, that work is added to the job.

Venting and condensate

Special venting, wall penetrations and a condensate drain are part of most gas installs.

Service type

A flush, a part replacement and a full install are different jobs with different time and materials.

Water quality and condition

A unit that has not been flushed for years may need more time or parts than one on a routine schedule.
