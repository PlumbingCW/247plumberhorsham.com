Signs the line from the street is failing

Wet or sunken ground

A soft strip of lawn, a sinking walk or a muddy patch with no rain points to a leak along the pipe route.

Pressure loss at every tap

If the whole house loses pressure at once, the problem is upstream of the branches, often at the main line or the pressure regulator.

Meter moves with everything off

Water leaving through a break registers on the meter even when no fixture is open.

Dirty or sputtering water

Air, grit or rust in the supply after a break or repair can come from a damaged line pulling in debris.

Bill jumps without explanation

A slow underground leak can run for weeks before the bill shows it.

No water or a flooded yard: what to do now

A failed service line is urgent because it can wash out soil, flood a basement wall or leave the house with no supply. Close the main valve inside, turn off the water heater if the house has no water, and call (215) 714-6692. We book same-day visits when available and take after-hours calls on nights and weekends.

Do not dig by hand near the suspected leak before the lines are marked. Pennsylvania requires homeowners and contractors to call 8-1-1 at least three business days before digging, and that wait matters because gas, electric and communication lines may share the route. Plan around it, and use the wait to shut the main and stage containers of water.

Who owns what: meter, curb stop and your pipe

The Horsham Water and Sewer Authority owns and maintains the line from its main in the street to the curb stop. You own the Building Water Service Line from the curb stop to the house, and its repair is your expense. That split decides who gets the first call, so find the curb stop (the Authority asks that it stay accessible) before any dig.

The meter is Authority property too, and it usually sits in the basement along the street-side wall. If water is surfacing between the street and the curb stop, call the Authority business office at (215) 672-8011. If it is surfacing between the curb stop and the house, the repair is yours, and we can start.

Finding the break without trenching the whole yard

A leak search can narrow a buried line to a few feet. Plumbers typically isolate the line, run pressure checks, and use acoustic gear to hear the leak. A dye or metal locator can follow the route when plans are missing.

That keeps the dig small. A short spot repair in one pit usually disturbs far less lawn and driveway than trenching the full length, and the trade-off is explained to you before work starts. For deeper detail on locating, see our leak detection page.

Repair or replace the service line

Spot repair makes sense when

  • The break is isolated and the pipe is otherwise sound
  • The pipe material is modern and in good shape
  • The route is easy to open in one place
  • You want the lowest disruption

Full replacement makes sense when

  • The line has failed more than once
  • Pipe is old steel, corroded or undersized
  • Pressure is weak even with no leak
  • You are already tearing up the yard

Typical replacement materials are copper and polyethylene or similar plastic water service tubing, and the right choice depends on soil, route and what the Authority requires. The Authority reports no known lead service lines, though its inventory lists most lines as unknown material. When the old pipe is exposed, have the plumber show you what it is made of and photograph it for your records.

How the repair work goes

  1. 1

    Locate and mark

    We confirm the line route, call for utility marking, and choose where to open the ground.

  2. 2

    Isolate and expose

    The authority or a valve upstream cuts supply as needed, and we open the pit around the break.

  3. 3

    Repair or replace

    We cut out the damaged pipe and install new service tubing with proper fittings, or run a longer replacement.

  4. 4

    Test and restore

    We pressure-test, flush the line, check pressure at the fixtures, and backfill. Restoring turf or paving is usually a separate trade.

Low pressure that is not a leak

Weak pressure does not always mean a break. A partly closed meter or main valve, a failing pressure-reducing valve, or old galvanized pipe clogged with scale can all do it. We check the valve, the regulator and the line before recommending digging.

If scaling points to a house-wide issue, the answer may be repiping rather than another repair on the street side. And when a split inside the house is the actual cause, see burst pipe repair.

Before we arrive: prepare for a line repair

Small steps save time on the day.

Locate the main and meter

Clear the path and tell us where the line enters the house.

Gather any paperwork

Old plot plans, prior repair notes or photos of the line help us trace the route.

Mark sprinkler heads and fences

Flag irrigation lines, invisible fences and landscaping that might be in the dig zone.

Plan for no water

Fill a few containers and store drinking water. Close the main to protect fixtures.

Where the plumbers focus on a water line job

The plumbers focus on the customer-side line from the curb stop to the house. The Authority handles its meter and its main in the street, and lawn or paving restoration is its own trade. Whether your project needs a township permit is a question for Code Enforcement at 1025 Horsham Road, (215) 643-3131, open Monday to Friday 8:30 AM to 4:30 PM.

Check the contractor as well as the pipe. Pennsylvania has no statewide plumber license for most construction work, but home improvement contractors doing $5,000 or more a year must register with the Attorney General. Look up any business at hicsearch.attorneygeneral.gov before you sign for a dig that will open your yard.
