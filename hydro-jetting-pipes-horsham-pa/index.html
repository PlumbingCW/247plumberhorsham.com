Hydro Jetting Your Pipes: A Guide for Horsham, PA Homes

Hydro jetting your pipes means scouring the inside of a drain line with a stream of water at pressures commonly between 1,500 and 4,000 psi. For Horsham, PA homes with repeat backups, it clears what a cable cannot: grease coating, scale, and the thin film that lets the next clog form fast.

It is not the right tool for every pipe, though. Below we cover what jetting does, which pipes are safe candidates, and how to prepare your house so the visit goes smoothly.

What hydro jetting does inside the pipe

A jetting hose carries a nozzle with several rear-facing jets and, usually, one forward jet. The rear jets push the nozzle up the pipe and also pull the hose along, while the water scrubs the pipe wall in a full circle. Debris is flushed downstream, rather than poked through a small hole as a cable does.

That difference matters because a drain cable cuts a channel the width of its head. Grease and soap scum on the sides of the pipe stay behind, narrowing the bore a little more each month. Jetting restores much of the original diameter.

Pipes and problems that suit it

Grease buildup in kitchen lines

Cooking fat cools into a waxy layer on the pipe wall. Jetting with heated or high-flow water strips it where a cable would only open a narrow path.

Tree roots in clay or cast iron laterals

A cutting nozzle can clear root masses at joints. Roots regrow through open joints, so jetting is maintenance unless the pipe is also repaired.

Scale and rust nodules in old cast iron

Cast iron grows rough internal deposits over decades. Jetting knocks the loose material off, as long as the pipe wall is sound.

Repeat clogs at the same spot

If a snaked line clogs again within weeks, the blockage is usually a coating rather than a plug.

When we say no to jetting

High pressure can finish off a pipe that is already cracked, collapsed, or badly offset. Fiber conduit sold under the Orangeburg name in the mid 1900s is especially fragile, and thin or corroded pipe may not survive the pressure either.

That is why a camera goes in first. If the picture shows damage, the better answer is a sewer line repair or a gentler cleaning method, not more pressure.

How a jetting visit runs

  1. 1

    Locate the cleanout

    The plumber finds your main cleanout, usually in the basement near the foundation wall or outside the house. Authority rules call for a cleanout at the right-of-way, at every bend and every 100 feet of building lateral, so ask which ones you have. If there is none, a toilet may have to be pulled.

  2. 2

    Run a camera

    A small camera shows pipe material, joints, offsets, roots, and cracks. This decides whether jetting is safe.

  3. 3

    Jet the line in passes

    The nozzle works from the cleanout toward the street, then back. Plumbers repeat passes until the water runs clear.

  4. 4

    Check flow and confirm

    Water is run through fixtures to test drainage, and a second look with the camera can show the cleaned wall.

What changes the price

Cost depends on line length, how many cleanouts there are, whether the camera inspection is bundled, and how much buildup the nozzle meets. Heavy root intrusion takes more passes than light grease. Cleaning a lateral with no cleanout adds labor to open access. Ask for the price before work starts, and ask whether the camera step is included or separate.

Keeping the line clean afterward

A freshly jetted line starts building film again the first time bacon grease goes down the kitchen sink. Wipe pans with a paper towel and put fat in the trash, since hot water only moves grease a few feet before it cools and sticks. Strainers in tub and shower drains catch hair before it reaches the main.

Flushable wipes are another repeat offender. They do not break down the way toilet paper does, and they snag on roots and rough joints in the lateral. Put them in the trash even when the package says otherwise.

After a jetting visit, watch for three signs over the next month. Gurgling in a toilet when the washing machine drains, slow drainage in the lowest fixture, or a sewer smell in the basement all mean the line is narrowing again or a section has shifted. Tell the plumber and ask for another camera pass rather than another round of pressure.

Preparing your house

Before the plumber arrives

  • Clear a path to the cleanout and move storage away from it.
  • Stop running water, dishwashers, and washing machines.
  • Lift rugs near the cleanout area and expect some water on the floor.
  • Keep pets and children out of the work area.
  • Note which fixtures drain slowly and when it began.

Who owns the pipe you are about to jet

Under the authority's Rules and Regulations (revised 2/9/26), Horsham Water & Sewer Authority owns the service lateral out to the right-of-way or easement line, and you own the building lateral from there to the house. Building laterals are supposed to have a cleanout at the right-of-way, at every bend, and every 100 feet. Count the cleanouts you have, because each one is a place a jetting hose can enter.

If a backup hits, call the authority at (215) 672-8011 before you book anyone. It checks the street main for free, and if the main is the problem, jetting your lateral would be money wasted. The authority also notes that basic homeowner policies often exclude sewer backups, so ask your insurance agent before you assume a cleaning bill is covered.

Businesses face a stricter rule. Grease, oil and sand interceptors are required in commercial and industrial buildings, the owner maintains them, and chemical dispersants need authority approval. If your kitchen line keeps coating over, jetting treats the symptom, so check that the interceptor is serviced. Debris from any job should leave through a sanitary cleanout, never a storm inlet, because the township's stormwater system drains to the Neshaminy, Wissahickon and Pennypack watersheds.
