Tank leaking or dead: the first 15 minutes

A leak from the tank body cannot be sealed, and the flow tends to grow as the steel gives way. Your priority is limiting the water, then calling for a replacement while the house is still manageable.

Replacement calls are often easier than they sound. If the old unit is the same fuel and a similar size, a plumber can usually remove it, set the new one and test it in a single visit. That is why we try to book these same day when we can.

Before we arrive: get ready for the swap

A few minutes of preparation keeps the visit short and the damage small.

Stop the water and the fuel

Close the cold valve at the top of the tank. Turn off the breaker for an electric unit or the gas control for a gas one. If you smell gas, go outside and call 911 or your gas utility (for PECO customers, 1-800-841-4141).

Drain only if it is safe

If the tank is hot, leave it. Opening the drain valve on a full, scalding tank sends water to the floor faster than a hose can carry it away.

Clear a path

Move storage away from the tank and the route from the door. A clear working area of a few feet saves time.

Find the label

Photograph the data plate with the model, capacity and fuel type. It helps us arrive with the right size and venting parts.

Replace now or repair first?

Repair usually makes sense when

  • The tank is not leaking and is on the younger side
  • The fault is a thermostat, element, thermocouple or valve
  • You plan to move or renovate soon
  • The warranty may still cover the tank

Replace usually makes sense when

  • Water is escaping from the tank shell or seam
  • The tank is old and rusting at the base or fittings
  • Sediment noise returns soon after flushing
  • The repair quote is close to the price of a new tank

If you are on the fence, our water heater repair team can diagnose it first and quote both options. Many homeowners spend a bit more on a swap when the old unit is past its best, because a failed tank causes the water damage.

40 gallon or 50 gallon: sizing the new tank

Size follows how many people draw hot water at the same time, not how many bedrooms you have. A 40 gallon tank suits many households of two to four people with staggered showers. A 50 gallon tank gives breathing room for larger families, a soaking tub or back-to-back laundry and dishwasher cycles.

Bigger is not always better. A larger tank takes up more floor, costs more to buy and keeps more water hot around the clock. We size by your actual use, and we check that the new tank fits the existing space and doorway.

What a new water heater costs and why quotes differ

What affects the price of a water heater replacement

Two quotes for the same house can differ for honest reasons. These are the main drivers.

Tank size and efficiency

A 50 gallon unit costs more than a 40 gallon one, and higher efficiency models or longer warranties add to the unit price.

Gas or electric, and any fuel change

Switching from electric to gas needs a gas line and a vent. Switching from gas to electric needs a dedicated circuit and enough panel capacity.

Venting and code updates

An old flue may not suit a new burner. Replacing or relining it is a separate job from the tank.

Expansion tank, valves and connectors

New shutoff valves, flex connectors, a relief valve and an expansion tank often make sense to replace with the tank.

Removal and permits

Hauling the old tank and the Township permit, which applies to water heaters, is part of the total.

Expansion tanks and relief valves: the parts people skip

When water heats, it expands. In a house where a backflow preventer or pressure-reducing valve on the incoming line stops that extra volume from pushing back into the street, the pressure has nowhere to go but your pipes and the tank. A small expansion tank absorbs it.

The temperature and pressure relief valve is the safety device on top of the tank, and its discharge pipe should run to a safe spot, not be capped. We test both when we set a new unit. Whether an expansion tank is required on your job depends on your water service and the manufacturer instructions, so we check and tell you.

Permits and inspections: ask Code Enforcement

The Township permit page says plumbing fixtures, including water heaters and water softeners, need a permit, and so does heating and AC replacement. Code Enforcement is at 1025 Horsham Road, (215) 643-3131, open Monday to Friday, 8:30 to 4:30. Ask them who files the permit for your job, and expect to give 24 to 48 hours notice for the inspection.

Pennsylvania has no statewide license for most construction contractors, and some municipalities license plumbers on their own, so check who you hire. Contractors doing $5,000 or more of home improvements a year must register with the Attorney General. You can look up any contractor, us included, at hicsearch.attorneygeneral.gov or by calling 1-888-520-6680 before work starts.

For gas units, the venting and gas piping must meet the requirements the township enforces, and Code Enforcement can tell you which inspection steps apply. We follow the manufacturer instructions, and we tell you if a flue or gas line needs correcting before the new tank goes in.

Gas, electric, tankless or heat pump: changing the type

Most emergency swaps keep the same fuel, because that is the fastest and cheapest path. If you want to change, a planned install is easier than a rush job, and we cover that on our water heater installation page. Tankless units need different gas supply and venting, which our tankless water heater page explains.

Heat pump water heaters pull heat from the surrounding air, so they need floor space, ceiling clearance and a drain for condensate. They work well in many basements. We discuss if yours suits one, but we do not push anyone away from a straight replacement when speed matters.
