Collapsed or broken sewer line: what to do now

Repair becomes urgent when waste cannot leave the house. A collapsed pipe stops flow entirely, and a broken one lets sewage seep into the soil beneath your yard or foundation. The first sign is usually a backup that returns right after a cleaning.

Before we arrive: keep the damage small

A failed sewer line gives you little warning, so protect the house while you wait.

Stop water use

No laundry, showers or dishwasher. Water that cannot drain goes to the lowest fixture in the house.

Avoid contact

Sewage in a basement is a health risk. Keep children and pets away and wear gloves if you must enter.

Do not dig

Do not start digging to find the pipe. Gas, electric and water lines may cross your yard, and Pennsylvania law requires homeowners and contractors to call 8-1-1 at least three business days before digging.

Photograph the damage

Pictures of wet areas, sunken ground or stains help with an insurance claim, if your policy covers it.

Signs a sewer line needs repair, not another cleaning

Backups that return fast

If a cleaning holds for days or weeks and then fails again, something in the pipe is catching debris.

Sunken or soggy ground

A depression or a persistently wet strip between the house and street can mean a leak below it.

Sewer odor outdoors or in the basement

Gas escapes through a crack or a disconnected joint. A smell near a floor drain or in the yard deserves a camera visit.

Rodents or insects near drains

Rats and cockroaches use broken pipes as tunnels into a house.

Slow drains in every fixture

Partial collapse shrinks the pipe. Water still passes but slowly, and the symptoms look like a clog.

Cast iron, clay and Orangeburg: what the camera usually finds

The pipe material shapes the repair. Cast iron was used widely in older homes and can corrode through the wall, which leaves flaking scale and thin spots. Clay tile sections are short, so there are many joints for roots to enter. Orangeburg, a pipe made of compressed tar paper, can soften, oval and collapse.

We tell you which one we see, with the footage, and we do not guess from the age of the house. A pipe that looks rough may still carry flow and may need only routine care, which is why a camera inspection comes before any quote for replacement.

Plastic, usually PVC or ABS, is common in newer lines. It resists corrosion and roots, but ground movement or a poor connection can still crack or separate it.

Trenchless repair or excavation: how we choose

Both methods work, and neither is always better. The camera, the depth of the pipe and what sits above it decide. A line under a driveway, a mature tree or a finished patio often favors trenchless methods. A collapsed or heavily bellied pipe often needs a trench.

Trenchless repair

  • Pipe lining adds a cured resin liner inside the existing pipe
  • Pipe bursting pulls a new pipe through as the old one is broken apart
  • Small access holes instead of a long trench
  • Needs a pipe that is open enough to run equipment
  • May not be possible if the pipe is collapsed or badly bellied

Excavation and replacement

  • Opens a trench along the failed section
  • Lets us fix collapsed pipe, bellies and bad connections directly
  • Lets us verify slope and bedding as we go
  • Disturbs lawn, walks or pavement that must be restored
  • Usually required where trenchless is not possible

Some customers ask about lining a line under a concrete slab. It can be possible when the pipe is accessible from a cleanout and not collapsed, and the camera decides. We would rather tell you it will not work than sell a liner that cannot cure properly.

Who owns the sewer lateral

The Horsham Water and Sewer Authority owns the service lateral up to the right-of-way or easement line. You own the building lateral from that line to the house and pay to maintain it. Before you approve a repair, have the camera mark where the failure sits relative to that line, because it decides who pays.

We can make the camera footage available to you, to the authority or to a second contractor, so you can confirm what is wrong. If the failure is in the Authority's section or the public main, call the Authority at (215) 672-8011 and we say so rather than quote you for it.

Authority rules also call for a cleanout on your lateral at the right-of-way, at every bend and every 100 feet, so we check for those and discuss adding one while the line is open.

How a repair goes from first call to finished pipe

  1. 1

    Camera and locate

    We record the line and mark the failure above ground, with its depth and distance from the cleanout.

  2. 2

  3. 3

    Marking and permits

    We call 8-1-1 for utility marking, which takes three business days, so we place the call early. Ask Horsham Township Code Enforcement at (215) 643-3131 whether your repair needs a permit, since the township fines work done before a required permit.

  4. 4

    Repair

    We line, burst or replace the failed section and connect it to the rest of the pipe.

  5. 5

    Prove it

    A final camera run and a water test show the repaired section is clear and flowing.

Pennsylvania's Uniform Construction Code adopts the International Plumbing Code for plumbing work, and we install pipe to the code that applies. We do not claim a permit is waived for any job, and we will tell you if the township asks for inspection.

Keep the old line running in the meantime

If the repair cannot happen today, a clearing may restore flow temporarily. A sewer line cleaning visit can buy time, but it is a stopgap if the pipe is cracked or collapsed. Water jetting, described on our hydro jetting page, is not safe on a broken pipe, which is why the camera comes first.

What affects the price of sewer line repair

Repair costs depend on the pipe, the method and the site. We give you a number before work starts.

Method

Lining, bursting and trench replacement involve different equipment, labor and restoration.

Length and depth

Deeper pipe and longer sections take more time and more material.

What is above the pipe

Driveways, patios, trees and landscaping affect the access and the restoration.

Pipe condition

A collapsed pipe limits trenchless choices, and a partly sound pipe offers more.

Permits and inspections

Code Enforcement asks for 24 to 48 hours notice before an inspection, which sets part of the schedule.
